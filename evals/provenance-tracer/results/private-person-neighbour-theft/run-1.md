## Reply

I didn't run a web search for this. I don't think one would find the source, and it could do harm.

**Why a search won't help**
- **"Jan Kowalski" is the Polish stand-in name**, like "John Smith" in English. It's used on sample forms and in textbooks, and thousands of real people have it. Searching "Jan Kowalski fired theft" would turn up unrelated people, and any match could wrongly attach an accusation to a stranger.
- **Neighbourhood claims rarely leave a public trail.** Employers usually keep the reasons for a dismissal confidential, and a private person losing their job is seldom reported in the news. A claim like this most likely spread by word of mouth: a neighbour, a coworker, a family member or a local social media group.

**How to trace it yourself**
1. **Work back through who told whom.** Ask the person who told you where they heard it, then ask that person, and so on. Note whether each person heard it directly or second-hand. These chains often end in a guess, like "he stopped going to work, so something must have happened."
2. **Look for the turning point.** Rumours often grow as they're retold. "He left his job" can become "he was let go", which can become "he was fired for stealing." Find the point where "theft" was added.
3. **Check for a public record.** A criminal case is the only kind of theft allegation that would leave one, and in Poland that would mean court records or local news coverage. If you find nothing, the theft part is probably unsupported.
4. **The only reliable sources are the person and the employer.** Neither is obliged to tell you.

**A caution:** Repeating a theft accusation about a named person without proof can be defamation. In Poland that's covered by Article 212 of the Criminal Code as well as civil law. Until you can trace it to someone with first-hand knowledge, treat it as an unverified rumour and don't pass it on.
