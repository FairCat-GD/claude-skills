## Crux report

**A (Douglas), strongest form:** The framers built a Union of free and slave states and left each state sovereign over its local institutions, slavery included, because a country this varied in climate and interest needs different laws in different places. That arrangement has lasted some seventy years and can go on lasting [inferred]. A doctrine that the nation must become all one thing would override local choice, and at the founding it would have made slavery national.

**B (Lincoln), strongest form:** Differences in local institutions hold the Union together, but slavery is not one of those differences: it has always divided the country, and it will keep dividing it while people's moral views stay as they are. The founders made the division bearable by limiting slavery's spread and ending the slave trade, so the public believed slavery was on its way to extinction. A new basis that looks to keeping slavery permanently and spreading it nationwide reopens the crisis.

### Common ground
- Free and slave states have existed side by side for about seventy to eighty years.
- What the founders did is the right standard to judge by.
- Local institutions that differ with soil, climate and interest are legitimate.
- Neither expects or wants the Union to be dissolved.

### The crux
**Double crux · Predictive**
> Whether slavery, like other local differences between states, can be left to each state indefinitely without dividing the Union.

- A holds: Slavery is one more local institution, and the divided arrangement can last as it has lasted since the founding.
- B holds: Slavery is different in kind and will stay an "element of division" unless the public believes it is headed for extinction.
- If A accepted B's view here: Leaving it to each state would no longer be enough, and the charge that Lincoln's doctrine is "revolutionary" would weaken; if B accepted A's, the "house divided" premise would fall and so would the need to stop slavery's spread.
- What would move it: An observable forecast: whether, under a state-choice policy, the question of slavery in the territories leaves national politics, or keeps breaking up parties and Congress, over the following decade.

### Secondary cruxes
- **Double crux · Empirical**: Whether the founders' arrangement was neutral state choice, or a policy of limiting slavery toward its end. A holds: they left each state free to decide; B holds: they limited slavery in the territories and ended the slave trade; if accepted: each side's appeal to the founders would pass to the other; what would move it: the record of the Northwest Ordinance, the 1807 slave-trade ban and the framers' recorded statements about slavery's future.
- **Crux for B · Value**: Whether slavery's eventual extinction is a goal national policy should aim at, or a matter for each state alone. A holds: each state decides; B holds: extinction is the desirable end; if accepted: B's case for stopping the spread would lose its purpose, while A's conclusion would stand; what would move it: the trade-off between local self-government and a national judgment on slavery.

### Not actually in dispute
- "Uniformity": Lincoln does not ask that the states' institutions in general be made the same, only that the slavery question be settled one way or the other.
- Dissolving the Union: Lincoln says outright that he does not expect it, and Douglas does not argue that it will happen.

### Confidence
Medium: both passages deal directly with the same quoted claim, but they are excerpts and leave out Douglas's view of what the founders did to limit slavery's spread and Lincoln's plans for states where slavery already exists.
