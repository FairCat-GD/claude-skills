## Crux report

**A (Douglas), strongest form:** The founders built the Union divided into free and slave states and left each state to decide slavery for itself, since varied climates and interests need varied laws. That arrangement has endured seventy years and can continue; a doctrine that the states must become all one thing is revolutionary.
**B (Lincoln), strongest form:** Local variety from soil and climate unites the Union, but slavery has always divided it and will while moral views persist. The founders restricted its spread and ended the slave trade, so the public believed it headed for extinction; restoring that restriction against the recent push toward nationalization would bring peace.

### Common ground
- The Union has lasted seventy to eighty years with some states slave and some free.
- Differences of soil, climate and interest rightly produce different local institutions.
- The founders' arrangement is the right standard for the present.

### The crux
**Double crux · Empirical**
> Whether the founders' arrangement, under which the divided Union lasted, only left slavery to each state, or also limited its spread so that it would decline over time.

- A holds: The founders left each state perfectly free to settle slavery within its own limits, and that alone is the principle the Union has lasted on.
- B holds: The founders also kept slavery out of new territories and cut off the slave trade, and that limit is why the arrangement lasted.
- If A accepted B's view here: A's appeal to the founders would support limiting slavery's spread, and A's case against B would weaken; if B accepted A's view, B's claim that the policy changed would lose its basis.
- What would move it: The record of the founding era, read side by side: the territorial ordinances, the slave-trade legislation, and the founders' own statements on slavery's expansion and future.

### Secondary cruxes
- **Double crux · Predictive**: Whether slavery, left as a local matter, can sit alongside free states without causing lasting conflict. A holds: it is one of many local laws suited to different places; B holds: it has always caused division and will keep doing so while moral views stay the same; if accepted: A would have to grant that the Union cannot last half and half on present terms, and B would lose the reason for urgency; what would move it: whether sectional conflict over slavery in the territories eases or grows by the 1860 election.

### Not actually in dispute
- Uniformity: B says plainly that variety in local institutions is a bond of union, so the dispute is only over slavery, not over making the states' institutions all the same.

### Confidence
Medium: both sides speak directly to the founders' arrangement, but these excerpts are short, and each side's reading of founding intent may depend on argument made elsewhere in the debate.
