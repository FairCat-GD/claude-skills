# Crux types

Every reported crux gets exactly one of five types. The type decides what the report says would
move it (procedure, step 8).

| Type | The disagreement is about | What would move it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Empirical | a checkable present or past fact | the specific evidence that would settle it |
| Predictive | a claim about future outcomes | an observable forecast with a date both could check |
| Definitional | one word, two meanings | two labelled terms replacing the shared word |
| Value | same facts, different weighting of goods | the trade-off, named; no resolution |
| Framing | the parties answer different questions | both questions, stated separately |

## How to classify

Ask these in order, and take the first yes:

1. **Are the parties answering different questions?** Their conclusions seem to clash, but their
   reasons answer two different questions, and no single claim of one contradicts a claim of the
   other. → **Framing.**
2. **Does one word carry two meanings,** and does a conclusion turn on which meaning is used?
   → **Definitional.**
3. **If both parties agreed on every fact and every forecast, would they still disagree?**
   → **Value.**
4. **Is it an outcome still to come, which no record yet could settle?** → **Predictive.** A
   claim about what has happened stays Empirical however it is worded: see *Empirical or
   Predictive* below.
5. **Otherwise:** a present or past fact that records, data or a measurement could check.
   → **Empirical.**

## Where two types seem to fit

- **Empirical or Predictive:** go by what would settle it, not by the tense of the sentence.
  What is or was, which a record could check, is Empirical; what will be, which no record yet
  settles, is Predictive. *Sales rose last year* is Empirical; *sales will keep rising* is
  Predictive. When the sides read what has happened differently (a history, or figures each
  reads its own way) and each forecast follows from its reading, the crux is that record, and
  it is Empirical: word its sentence in the past or present. When they agree on the record and
  differ only on what will follow from it, the crux is Predictive.
- **Definitional, or not in dispute at all:** split the word into two labelled terms. If both
  parties then agree on both terms and each conclusion still stands, the disagreement dissolves:
  it goes under *Not actually in dispute*. It is a Definitional crux only when a conclusion
  still turns on which meaning applies.
- **Value or a fact underneath it:** when one side's weighting rests on a fact the other
  disputes (that the path is dangerous), that fact is the deeper crux, and it is Empirical or
  Predictive. A Value crux is what is left when the facts are shared.
- **Framing or Value:** in a Value crux both sides answer the same question and weigh its goods
  differently; in a Framing crux they answer different questions.

## Examples

Every example is fictional. Each gives the two positions, the crux as the report would state it,
why it is this type, and what would move it.

### Empirical

**1. The boiler.** Two tenants at a building meeting.

- A holds: the building needs a new boiler, because it broke down more often this winter than
  last.
- B holds: a repair will do, because this winter's breakdowns were no more frequent than last
  winter's.
- **Double crux · Empirical**
  > Whether the boiler broke down more often this winter than last winter.
- Why Empirical: a past fact that the building's records can check.
- What would move it: the maintenance log and the repair invoices for both winters, with a
  breakdown counted the same way in each, such as a call-out that left the flats without heat.

**2. The crossing.** Two parents on the school's parent committee.

- A holds: the school should keep paying for a crossing guard at the corner, because near-misses
  there have fallen since the guard started.
- B holds: the money would do more elsewhere, because near-misses at the corner were already
  rare before the guard started.
- **Double crux · Empirical**
  > Whether near-misses at the corner have fallen since the crossing guard started.
- Why Empirical: a past fact; both sides' conclusions follow from what the record shows.
- What would move it: the school's incident log and the council's traffic reports for the
  corner, for a year before the guard started and the year since, counted the same way.

### Predictive

**1. Online rehearsals.** Two members of a choir's committee.

- A holds: rehearsals should move online for the winter, because most members will keep coming
  if they do not have to travel.
- B holds: rehearsals should stay in the hall, because attendance online will fall off within a
  few weeks.
- **Double crux · Predictive**
  > Whether attendance at online rehearsals would hold up through the winter.
- Why Predictive: about a future outcome; no record yet exists.
- What would move it: a forecast both can check: if rehearsals run online from January, average
  attendance by the end of February is within a tenth of last February's in the hall, or it is
  not.

**2. The export module.** Two developers on a small team.

- A holds: the export module should be rewritten before the new file format is added, because
  patched, it will keep breaking.
- B holds: a patch is enough, because once the known bug is fixed the module will hold up.
- **Double crux · Predictive**
  > Whether the export module, once patched, will need further urgent fixes before the end of
  > the quarter.
- Why Predictive: about how the module will behave from here on.
- What would move it: the team's issue tracker on the last day of the quarter: the number of
  urgent fixes to the patched module since the patch, against a number both name now.

### Definitional

**1. A flat route.** Two organisers of a running club.

- A holds: the Sunday route should be listed as flat for beginners, because it has hardly any
  total climb.
- B holds: it should not be listed as flat, because it has one short, steep hill.
- **Double crux · Definitional**
  > Whether "flat" in the club's listing means little total climb or no steep stretch.
- Why Definitional: both accept the facts, low total climb and one steep hill; they use one word
  for two things, and the listing turns on which.
- What would move it: two labelled terms, *low-climb* (little total ascent) and *no-steep*
  (no stretch above a set gradient). Each side can then state which the beginners' listing
  should require.

**2. Local news.** Two editors at a town paper.

- A holds: the story belongs in the local section, because it happened in town.
- B holds: it belongs in the national section, because it is about a national company's
  decision, not about the town.
- **Double crux · Definitional**
  > Whether "local news" means news that happened in the town or news about the town.
- Why Definitional: both agree where the story happened and what it is about; the section turns
  on the word.
- What would move it: two labelled terms, *local-by-place* and *local-by-subject*, so the
  section's rule can name which one it uses.

### Value

**1. The bandstand or the footpaths.** Two trustees of a village fund, which can pay for one of
the two this year.

- A holds: the fund should restore the bandstand, because it is the village's last old structure
  and could not be rebuilt once it falls.
- B holds: the fund should repair the footpaths, because people use them every day.
- Both accept what each job costs and how worn each is.
- **Double crux · Value**
  > Whether preserving the bandstand or repairing the footpaths should come first, with the
  > costs and conditions of both agreed.
- Why Value: same facts; each side weighs a different good more.
- What would move it: the trade-off, named: a structure that cannot be replaced against the
  everyday use of the paths. No resolution is offered.

**2. Hours or books.** Two members of a library's committee, with one sum to spend.

- A holds: the library should open two evenings a week, because more people could use it.
- B holds: the money should go on books, because a library is only as good as its shelves.
- Both accept what the evenings would cost and how many books that sum would buy.
- **Double crux · Value**
  > Whether the same money is better spent on more open hours or on more books.
- Why Value: same facts and the same question, weighted differently.
- What would move it: the trade-off, named: when people can come against what they find when
  they do. No resolution is offered.

### Framing

**1. The shed.** Two members of a community garden's committee.

- A holds: the garden should not build a shed this year, because this year's funds do not cover
  it.
- B holds: the garden should build a shed, because the tools are rusting outside.
- A does not dispute the rust, and B does not dispute the funds.
- **Double crux · Framing**
  > Whether the decision turns on what the garden can pay for this year or on what it needs.
- Why Framing: no claim conflicts; A answers whether the garden can pay for a shed this year,
  and B answers whether it needs one, and each conclusion would change if its side took up the
  other's question.
- What would move it: both questions, stated separately: Do we need a shed? Can we pay for one
  this year?

**2. The kitchen.** Two flatmates.

- A holds: the cleaning rota should change, because its tasks are not split evenly.
- B holds: the rota is fine as it is, because the kitchen is clean enough.
- Neither disputes the other's reason.
- **Double crux · Framing**
  > Whether the rota should be judged by how evenly it splits the work or by how clean the
  > kitchen is.
- Why Framing: A answers whether the split is even, and B answers whether the kitchen is clean
  enough; both answers can be true at once.
- What would move it: both questions, stated separately: Is the split of tasks even? Is the
  kitchen clean enough?
